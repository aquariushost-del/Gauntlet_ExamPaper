## 1. Assessment structure

### Official assessment for syllabus 5086

| Paper | Coverage | Duration | Marks | Weighting of combined subject |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | Physics and Chemistry MCQ | 1 hour | 40 | 20.0% |
| 2 | Physics structured and free response | 1 hour 15 minutes | 65 | 32.5% |
| 3 | Chemistry structured and free response | 1 hour 15 minutes | 65 | 32.5% |
| 5 | Physics and Chemistry practical | 1 hour 30 minutes | 30 | 15.0% |

**Paper 1:** 40 compulsory MCQs, with approximately equal coverage of the two sciences. A Physics-only 20-question paper is a school adaptation; the official paper contains both sciences.

**Paper 2:**

- Section A: 55 marks; all structured questions compulsory; the last question carries 10 marks.
- Section B: two questions, each worth 10 marks; candidates answer one.
- Candidate total: 65 marks. The two alternatives mean 75 marks of question material are authored across the paper.
- The syllabus does not prescribe a fixed number of Section A questions.

**Paper 5:** One or two compulsory questions on each science. Candidates suggest a modification or extension in one or more questions without having to execute it. This element accounts for 10%-20% of the total practical-test marks, depending on context. Do not confuse the combined 30-mark paper with a school Physics-only practical.

### Assessment objectives

| Objective | Key expectations | Weighting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: Knowledge with Understanding | Facts, laws, definitions, concepts, terminology, symbols, units, apparatus, safety, determination of quantities, and applications and their implications | Approximately 50% of theory marks; approximately 20% of theory marks allocated to recall |
| B: Handling Information and Solving Problems | Select and organise information; translate representations; manipulate data; infer patterns; explain relationships; predict; propose hypotheses; solve problems | Approximately 50% of theory marks |
| C: Experimental Skills and Investigations | Follow instructions; use apparatus; observe, measure and estimate; interpret results; plan investigations; evaluate methods and improvements | Assessed through Paper 5 |

Unfamiliar contexts can assess Objective B, provided candidates can reason using syllabus principles and the information supplied. Command words alone do not determine the assessment objective.

## 2. Physics framework

The six disciplinary ideas are:

1. The Universe consists of matter and energy.
2. Matter interacts through forces and fields.
3. Forces explain motion.
4. Waves transfer energy without transferring matter.
5. Conservation laws constrain changes in systems.
6. Microscopic models explain macroscopic phenomena.

Scientific practice includes questioning, investigation design, experimentation, data analysis, evidence-based explanation, modelling and informed decisions. Scientific knowledge is evidence-based and open to revision; applications have social, ethical, economic and environmental implications (pp. 3-4, 8).

## 3. Topic-by-topic learning outcomes

### I. Measurement

#### 1. Physical Quantities, Units and Measurement (p. 10)

- **1(a):** Understand that a physical quantity typically has a numerical magnitude and a unit.
- **1(b):** Recall base quantities and units: mass (kg), length (m), time (s), current (A), temperature (K), amount of substance (mol).
- **1(c):** Use SI prefixes nano (n), micro (µ), milli (m), centi (c), deci (d), kilo (k), mega (M), giga (G), tera (T).
- **1(d):** Understand orders of magnitude for common objects, from a typical atom to Earth.
- **1(e):** Select and justify measuring instruments using their range and precision, for quantities in the syllabus quantity list.
- **1(f):** Define scalar and vector quantities and give common examples.

### II. Newtonian Mechanics

#### 2. Kinematics (p. 11)

- **2(a)-(b):** Define speed and velocity; calculate average speed from distance travelled and time taken.
- **2(c)-(d):** Define uniform acceleration, calculate acceleration from change in velocity/time, and interpret non-uniform acceleration.
- **2(e)-(f):** Plot and interpret distance-time graphs for one-direction motion; identify rest, uniform speed and non-uniform speed.
- **2(g):** Interpret speed-time graphs to identify rest, uniform speed, uniform acceleration and non-uniform acceleration.
- **2(h):** Use area under speed-time graphs to find distance for one-direction motion with uniform speed or uniform acceleration.
- **2(i):** Recall that free-fall acceleration near Earth is constant and approximately 10 m/s².

#### 3. Force and Pressure (p. 12)

- **3(a):** Distinguish contact forces (friction, air resistance, tension, normal force) from non-contact forces (gravitational, electrostatic, magnetic).
- **3(b):** State that mass measures the amount of matter in a body.
- **3(c)-(d):** Describe a gravitational field and define gravitational field strength as gravitational force per unit mass at a point.
- **3(e)-(f):** Apply weight = mass × gravitational field strength; distinguish mass from weight.
- **3(g):** Apply density = mass/volume.
- **3(h)-(i):** Define pressure and apply pressure = force/area.

#### 4. Dynamics (pp. 12-13)

- **4(a):** Apply Newton's laws to balanced/unbalanced forces, changes in motion and action-reaction pairs on two interacting bodies. **Formal statements of Newton's laws are not required.**
- **4(b):** Identify forces and draw free-body diagrams involving forces in at most two dimensions.
- **4(c):** Apply resultant force = mass × acceleration.
- **4(d):** Explain how friction affects motion.

#### 5. Turning Effects of Forces (p. 13)

- **5(a):** Describe moments as turning effects about a pivot, with everyday examples.
- **5(b):** Apply moment = force × perpendicular distance from pivot.
- **5(c)-(d):** State and apply the principle of moments for a body in equilibrium.
- **5(e):** Understand that weight can be treated as acting at the centre of gravity.

#### 6. Energy (pp. 13-14)

- **6(a):** Understand kinetic, gravitational/chemical/elastic potential, nuclear and internal energy stores. Describe transfers mechanically, electrically, by heating and by electromagnetic or mechanical waves.
- **6(b):** Apply kinetic energy = ½mv² and gravitational potential energy near Earth = mgh.
- **6(c):** State and apply conservation of energy.
- **6(d):** Apply work done = force × distance moved in the force's direction.
- **6(e):** Apply power = energy transfer/time.

### III. Thermal Physics

#### 7. Kinetic Particle Model of Matter (p. 15)

- **7(a)-(b):** Compare solids, liquids and gases; explain their properties using particle arrangement, motion, forces and separation.
- **7(c):** Relate increasing temperature to increasing average particle kinetic energy.
- **7(d):** Describe internal energy as the total kinetic energy of random particle motion plus the total potential energy between particles.
- **7(e):** Describe melting/solidification and boiling/condensation as energy-transfer processes without temperature change.

#### 8. Thermal Processes (p. 16)

- **8(a):** Explain energy transfer by heating from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium.
- **8(b):** Explain conduction in solids microscopically through atomic/molecular vibrations and electron movement.
- **8(c):** Explain convection in fluids through density changes.
- **8(d):** Explain that electromagnetic radiation needs no material medium; relate transfer rate to surface colour/texture, temperature and area.
- **8(e):** Apply conduction, convection and radiation to everyday contexts.

### IV. Waves

#### 9. General Properties of Waves (pp. 17-18)

- **9(a)-(b):** Describe wave motion using ropes, springs and ripple tanks, including wavefronts; understand energy transfer without matter transfer.
- **9(c):** Define and use wave speed, frequency, wavelength, period and amplitude, including graphical representations.
- **9(d):** Apply wave speed = frequency × wavelength.
- **9(e):** Compare transverse and longitudinal waves and give examples.
- **9(f)-(g):** Explain vibrating sound sources, the need for a medium, and longitudinal propagation through compressions and rarefactions.
- **9(h):** Relate loudness to amplitude and pitch to frequency.
- **9(i):** Explain echoes and their use in measuring distance.

#### 10. Electromagnetic Spectrum (p. 18)

- **10(a):** State that all electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at the same speed in vacuum.
- **10(b):** Order the spectrum by wavelength and frequency.
- **10(c):** Give typical uses of all seven regions.
- **10(d):** Describe hazards from over-exposure, including heating and ionisation effects on cells and tissue.

In increasing frequency and decreasing wavelength: **radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma**.

| Region | Examples given in the syllabus |
| --- | --- |
| Radio | Radio/TV communication, astronomy, RFID tags |
| Microwave | Mobile phones, microwave ovens, satellite TV |
| Infrared | Remote controls, intruder alarms, thermal imaging |
| Visible | Photography, optical fibres in medicine and telecommunications |
| Ultraviolet | Sunbeds, banknote authentication, water disinfection |
| X-ray | Medical radiology, security screening, industrial defect detection |
| Gamma | Food sterilisation, cancer detection and treatment |

#### 11. Light (pp. 18-19)

- **11(a)-(b):** Use normal, angle of incidence and angle of reflection; state and apply equality of incidence/reflection angles in construction, measurement and calculation.
- **11(c)-(d):** Use refraction terminology; apply sin i/sin r = constant.
- **11(e):** Define refractive index as speed of light in vacuum divided by speed in the medium.
- **11(f)-(g):** Describe the action of a thin converging lens on a light beam and define its focal length.
- **11(h):** Describe images as real/virtual, magnified/diminished and upright/inverted. **Recall of image characteristics for specific object distances is not required.**

### V. Electricity and Magnetism

#### 12. Electric Charge and Current of Electricity (p. 20)

- **12(a)-(b):** State positive/negative charge, the coulomb unit, attraction of unlike charges and repulsion of like charges.
- **12(c)-(d):** Define current as charge flow rate, with ampere units; distinguish conventional current from electron flow.
- **12(e):** Apply charge = current × time.
- **12(f):** State that e.m.f. is measured in volts. The quantity summary also requires its definition.
- **12(g):** Define p.d. as work done per unit charge in driving charge through a component; use volts.
- **12(h)-(i):** State and apply resistance = p.d./current.
- **12(j):** Apply resistance proportional to wire length and inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.

#### 13. D.C. Circuits (p. 21)

- **13(a):** Draw circuit diagrams with cells/batteries, switches, lamps, LEDs, fixed/variable resistors, fuses, ammeters and voltmeters.
- **13(b)-(c):** Apply equal current throughout a series circuit and addition of series potential differences.
- **13(d)-(e):** Apply addition of parallel branch currents and equal p.d. across parallel branches.
- **13(f):** Apply equivalent-resistance formulae for series and parallel combinations.
- **13(g):** Combine current, p.d. and resistance relationships to solve whole-circuit problems.

#### 14. Practical Electricity (pp. 21-22)

- **14(a):** Describe the heating effect in appliances such as kettles, ovens and heaters.
- **14(b):** Apply P = VI and E = VIt.
- **14(c):** Calculate appliance costs using energy in kWh.
- **14(d):** Describe hazards of damaged insulation, overheating cables and damp conditions.
- **14(e):** Explain fuses, circuit breakers and fuse ratings.
- **14(f):** Explain earthing metal casings and double insulation.
- **14(g)-(h):** State meanings of live, neutral and earth; describe mains-plug wiring.
- **14(i):** Explain why switches, fuses and circuit breakers are fitted in the live wire.

#### 15. Magnetism and Electromagnetism (p. 22)

- **15(a):** State magnet properties.
- **15(b):** Describe induced magnetism near a strong magnet or inside a current-carrying solenoid.
- **15(c):** Distinguish temporary magnets (e.g. iron) from permanent magnets (e.g. steel), including uses.
- **15(d):** Use a compass/bar magnet to determine field direction.
- **15(e):** Draw fields around a bar magnet and between two bar-magnet poles.
- **15(f):** Draw fields around straight current-carrying wires and solenoids; explain changes when current magnitude/direction changes.
- **15(g):** Describe experiments showing force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field and the effects of reversing current or field.
- **15(h):** Use Fleming's left-hand rule to deduce relative force, field and current directions when any two are perpendicular.
- **15(i):** Explain the turning effect on a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field. **Recall of an electric motor's structure is not required.**

### VI. Radioactivity

#### 16. Radioactivity (pp. 23-24)

- **16(a):** Describe the atom's positively charged nucleus containing protons/neutrons and its negatively charged electrons.
- **16(b)-(c):** Use proton/atomic number Z, nucleon/mass number A, isotope and nuclide; interpret notation with A at upper left and Z at lower left of element symbol X.
- **16(d):** Explain nuclear decay as random and spontaneous, with unstable nuclei losing energy through radiation emission.
- **16(e):** Understand alpha, beta and gamma radiation, including ionising effects and penetration. **Only beta-minus particles are assumed.**
- **16(f):** Understand background radiation.
- **16(g):** Use half-life in simple calculations, including tables and decay curves.
- **16(h):** State applications, including medical/industrial uses, and hazards.

## 4. Key mathematical relationships

These are relationships explicitly required by the outcomes, written in conventional notation. Define symbols clearly where they could be confused.

| Topic | Relationship | Note |

| Power | P = E/t | Energy transfer rate |

## 6. Physics practical requirements (pp. 55-57)

The following list is **not exhaustive**. Theory papers may refer to techniques used in these experiments, but detailed descriptions of experimental procedures are not required by this practical-assessment statement.

### Measurement techniques

- Length: tapes, rules, digital calipers and digital micrometers.
- Time intervals, including a simple pendulum's period: digital stopwatches.
- Solid/liquid volume: measuring cylinders.
- Mass and weight: electronic balances and spring balances respectively.
- Temperature: laboratory thermometers.
- Current and voltage: ammeters and voltmeters.

### Investigations identified in the syllabus

1. Density of liquids and regularly/irregularly shaped solids that sink in water.
2. Acceleration of free fall.
3. Effects of balanced and unbalanced forces.
4. Principle of moments.
5. Centre of gravity of a plane lamina.
6. Factors affecting thermal energy transfer.
7. Law of reflection.
8. Position and characteristics of images formed by a plane mirror or thin converging lens.
9. Refraction through glass blocks.
10. Resistance of a circuit component.

### Apparatus highlights

- Electrical: analogue ammeters/voltmeters, cells, lamps, rheostat, resistors, switches, leads, resistance wires, bar magnets and compass.
- Mechanics: pendulum bob, stands/clamps, pivot, pulley, newton-meters, rules, digital calipers/micrometer, stopwatch, balance, springs and slotted masses.
- Thermal: thermometer, beakers, boiling tube, measuring cylinders, insulating cup, safe water-heating equipment and stirrer.
- Optical: glass block, plane mirror, converging lens (listed focal length 15 cm), lens holder, screen, torch, protractor, pin board, pins and tracing paper.

The full apparatus list is on p. 57. It is guidance, not an exhaustive list; actual requirements vary annually and centres receive advance notice. Spare sets are required for breakages and malfunctions.

## 7. Command words and examination conventions

| Command | Expected response, summarised from pp. 63-64 |
| --- | --- |
| Calculate | Numerical answer; show working, particularly for multiple steps |
| Compare | Similarities and differences |
| Define | Formal definition or equivalent paraphrase |
| Describe | Main features in words, with diagrams where appropriate |
| Determine | Often derive from measured/known quantities rather than measure directly |
| Estimate | Reasoned order-of-magnitude answer, with appropriate simplifying assumptions |
| Explain | Reasoning and/or relevant theory |
| Measure | Obtain directly from a measuring instrument |
| Outline | Essential points briefly |
| Predict / deduce | Logical connection from information rather than recall; predict generally calls for a concise answer |
| Sketch | Qualitatively correct graph or clear, proportionate diagram; relevant quantitative features may matter |
| State | Concise answer with little supporting argument |
| Suggest | Possible answer or application to a novel context; a unique answer may not exist |
| What is meant by | Definition plus relevant contextual comment where warranted by marks |

- Units must be supplied where required; dimensionless ratios should not have units.
- Inappropriate significant figures or misuse of units can be penalised. The source does not impose a single universal significant-figure rule.
- An approved calculator may be used in all papers.
- Geometrical instruments are required for Papers 1 and 2 in syllabus 5086.

## 8. Scope checks when setting or reviewing questions

The following checks are derived from this extraction; they are not additional official syllabus requirements.

- Map each assessed concept to a topic outcome, the quantity list or practical requirements.
- Include Radioactivity when claiming coverage of the full Physics syllabus.
- Include Measurement unless the school explicitly excludes it for that assessment.
- Respect the stated limits: formal Newton's-law statements, recall of lens images at specified object distances, and recall of motor structure are not required; beta radiation is beta-minus only.
- Use energy-store and energy-transfer language consistent with Topic 6.
- Do not automatically import content from Pure Physics. For example, specific heat capacity/latent heat equations, total internal reflection/critical-angle calculations, thin-lens equations, electromagnetic induction and transformers are not explicit learning outcomes in this document. Supplied-information problems must remain solvable with in-syllabus principles.
- Treat the quantity table as part of the examinable expectations, including definitions marked with an asterisk.
- For an official-format Paper 2, check Section A = 55, final Section A question = 10, Section B = two 10-mark alternatives, candidate total = 65.
- Aim for approximately equal Knowledge with Understanding and Handling Information/Solving Problems across theory assessment, with approximately 20% of theory marks for recall.
- Use the specimen paper separately for visual formatting. The syllabus defines content and assessment structure; it does not specify all font, spacing or page-layout details.
